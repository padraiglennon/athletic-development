# What the GAA action statement says, in plain English

Source: `gaelic-games-action-statement-2024.pdf`, published by the GAA, the Ladies Gaelic Football Association and the Camogie Association in January 2024.
Online at https://learning.gaa.ie/sites/default/files/2024-01/Gaelic%20Games%20-%20Action%20statement.pdf

The original is nineteen pages and written for people with sports science degrees. This is the same content in ordinary words, with a section at the end on what it means for our U11s.

## What this document is and is not

It is a set of principles. It is not a set of drills. The document says so itself: it gives "a framework for action" and deliberately does not tell you what to do on a Tuesday night. There are no age groups in it, no exercises, no tests. It promises that practical coaching resources will follow later.

So do not go looking for a session plan in it. What it gives you is a way of deciding what belongs in a session and what does not.

It was written by a mix of university researchers and working coaches, including people from Setanta College, Dublin City University, and the Cork, Dublin, Limerick and Donegal county setups.

## The two things it is trying to achieve

1. Help every player enjoy Gaelic games for as long as they live.
2. Help develop players who can eventually play for their county.

In that order. The document says a programme should be judged on both, and the first one is listed first on purpose. A squad that produces one county minor and loses fourteen boys to other sports has failed by this measure.

## What "athleticism" means here

The document defines it as being able to do a range of movements, over and over, accurately and confidently, in all sorts of situations.

The important words are "over and over" and "all sorts of situations". A boy who can do a tidy squat in the warm-up but falls over when someone bumps into him during a game has not got there yet.

## The three parts of the picture

The framework is drawn as a Celtic knot with three loops and a centre.

| Loop | In plain words |
| --- | --- |
| Force | How well he can push, pull, hold a shape, and absorb a landing |
| Gamespeed | How well he moves. Starting, running, stopping, turning, changing direction |
| Endurance | Whether he can still do all of that in the last ten minutes, and in September |

In the middle sits the game itself, and two outcomes. On one side, health and staying involved for life. On the other, playing well.

Drawing it as a knot is the point. The three loops overlap. You cannot change direction sharply without being able to absorb force, and you cannot keep changing direction late in a game without some fitness behind it.

Four things sit around the outside and govern how you use it:

- Understand that boys grow and mature at different rates
- Fit in with the club and county setup rather than working around it
- Plan properly
- Include everyone

There is a line in it worth remembering. Players have to "be physically competent to do the technical stuff, and technically competent to do the tactical stuff". A boy who cannot get his body into position cannot execute the skill, and a boy who cannot execute the skill cannot make the right decision.

## Do it well before you do it hard

This is the most useful instruction in the whole document, and it repeats in every section.

The order is: **find the shape, hold the shape, move in and out of the shape, cope when the shape is challenged, and only then add weight.**

Weight comes last, and it only comes when the first four are solid. The document is blunt that this is decided by how well a boy moves, not by how old he is. Players "earn the right" to move on. If his movement gets worse, you went too fast, and you go back a step.

## Four stages, in each of the three areas

Every one of the three areas runs through the same four stages. Our U11s are at stage one in all three. That is not a limitation, it is the entire job at this age.

**Force**
1. Can he do it well? Find the shape, hold it, move in and out of it.
2. Can he do it well with a bit of weight? Small increases only.
3. Can he do it well, with weight, and quickly?
4. Can he do it at the level senior county football demands?

**Gamespeed**
1. Can he do a wide range of movements well? Starting, running, stopping, turning.
2. Can he join those movements together into something that looks like sport?
3. Can he use them in a real situation, while also looking, thinking and deciding?
4. Can he do all of it at full pace in a match?

**Endurance**
1. Does he move well, and can he keep going at a basic level?
2. Has he built up general fitness?
3. Has he built the specific fitness the game demands?
4. Has he got what a top-level game requires?

## Notes on each of the three areas

**Force.** The document says weight training is safe and good for young players when it is set up properly, and it argues for it on health grounds as much as performance grounds, pointing at bone strength and general health. The performance argument is simply that every start, stop and turn in a game comes from pushing against the ground, so a boy who can push harder can move better. None of that means you put weight on a nine year old. The order still applies: do it well first.

**Gamespeed.** Movement is split into starting, running, and changing. The idea is to give a boy a wide vocabulary of movements first, then gradually make each one harder by giving him more to think about. Single movements become combinations. General movements become football or hurling movements. Predictable drills become unpredictable ones. The document makes a fair complaint here: coaches tend to think "movement" means squats and lunges and forget that running, stopping and turning are skills that have to be taught.

**Endurance.** The recommended method is getting fit by playing rather than by running. Small games with fewer players on a smaller pitch are named specifically, with Irish research behind them in hurling. Two warnings come with it. Boys need some basic fitness before a game can push them hard enough to improve them, and at older ages small games will not be hard enough on their own. Neither warning matters much at U11.

## Growing and maturing

The document says plainly that Gaelic games has a bias towards bigger, earlier-developing boys, and that this needs fixing. What it asks for:

- Measure every player's height and weight at the start of the season, and again every four to six months.
- Record height to the nearest tenth of a centimetre and weight to the nearest tenth of a kilo.
- Always have two adults present.
- Take their date of birth, and their parents' heights if the parents are happy to give them.
- Tell the boys and their parents clearly that this is about keeping them safe, not about picking a team.

From those few numbers you can tell how fast a boy is growing, whether he is ahead of or behind his age group, whether a growth spurt is starting, and roughly how tall he will end up. The growth spurt matters most, because that is when injury risk goes up.

It also asks coaches to watch out for boys born late in the year. A boy born in December is competing against boys nearly a year older, and at ten years old that is an enormous gap. The document wants that actively corrected, not just noticed.

## Keeping an eye on how much work they are doing

There are two sides to this. How much work a boy actually did, and how hard it felt to him. The same session lands very differently on different boys.

The method the document recommends costs nothing. After every session the player rates it from 1 to 10, where 1 is nothing at all and 10 is the hardest he could go. Multiply that by how long the session lasted in minutes. A 75 minute session rated 6 gives you 450. That number means nothing on its own, but tracked week to week it tells you when you have suddenly asked for more than usual. The document calls this session RPE if you want to look it up.

The one warning: boys tend to rate the session on the hardest bit or the last bit, so the number is rough. It is still better than guessing.

On sudden jumps: unless there is a very good reason, the total should not go up by more than about a tenth from one week to the next. It does need to go up over months and years. It just should not jump.

## The eight rules of planning

Taken straight from the document, because this is the part most likely to be skipped.

1. Know everything a boy is doing, not just what he does with you. Club, school, county, other sports.
2. Help the boy doing too much, and help the boy doing too little.
3. Watch for sudden jumps in how much he is doing. That is when boys get hurt.
4. Ease off before a big game.
5. Avoid two hard sessions in one day, two full matches inside 60 hours, and hard sessions on back to back days.
6. Leave gaps in the week for messing about and playing for the sake of it.
7. Explain all of this to the boys, and get them telling you when they are tired.
8. Encourage them to play other sports. Ring the other coaches and sort the week out between you.

Rule 8 puts the work on the coach, not the child. If a boy has soccer on Tuesday and hurling on Thursday, picking up the phone to the soccer coach is our job.

## The player pathway

The document maps out how a player progresses, with the club at the centre of it.

- First, basic movement skills
- Then, extending and refining those movements
- Then, committing to Gaelic games and an active life
- Then four stages of talent development, from showing promise to breaking through
- Finally, senior county player

You can join at any point. That matters, because it means a boy who takes up hurling at fourteen is not written off. He starts at the same place everyone starts, doing things well before doing them hard.

## What to call the coach

The document asks that anyone doing this work be called an **athletic development coach** rather than a strength and conditioning coach. Its reasoning is that the strength and conditioning label came from American football and makes parents picture a gym and a set of weights, which is the wrong picture entirely for a child.

## What all of this means for our U11s

Roughly nine to eleven years old. Nearly all of them are still before their growth spurt. The document puts them at the very start of the pathway, at stage one of all three areas.

Our sessions already fit the framework without changing anything. The match rotation is the fitness. The skills rotation is the hurling and football. The athletic development rotation is the movement and the body shapes. Three rotations, three parts of the knot.

- **Basic movement skills are the job this year.** Not fitness, not strength. The document says this directly for children of this age.
- **No weights. None.** Nothing heavier than their own body. To make something harder, slow it down, do it on one leg, add a rule, or ask for more control. Never add weight.
- **Fitness comes from playing.** No laps, no shuttle runs. Small games on a small pitch, with the numbers and the space adjusted to make it harder or easier.
- **Teach them to stop and turn.** Landing, stopping, and changing direction are skills. Boys do not pick them up on their own. This is the weakest part of most juvenile coaching and it is also where injuries come from.
- **Games alone will not do it.** The document is direct about this. A game develops some boys and leaves others standing around. You have to add proper movement work on top.
- **We are not measuring or testing anyone.** The document does ask for height and weight twice a year, and we have decided against it at U11. It is worth doing at U13 and U14, when boys start growing at very different rates.
- **Push other sports.** Genuinely. And protect the free time.
- **Fun is part of the design.** The document quotes the old line, "Mol an óige agus tiocfaidh sí", praise youth and she will thrive. A session that is well planned and joyless has still failed.

## What the document does not tell you

- No ages are attached to any stage, on purpose, because it all depends on the individual boy. You have to judge your own squad.
- No drills, no exercises, no tests, no session structures.
- Nothing about how many boys one coach can handle, which is the thing that actually decides what you can run on a wet Thursday.
